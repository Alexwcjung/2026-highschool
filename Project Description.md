# Project Description

## 🎯 Teaching Context

This project was designed for Korean vocational high school students. Although the learners are high school students, many of them have English proficiency around the elementary to early middle school level. They often struggle with listening, pronunciation, basic vocabulary, and confidence in speaking English.

The classroom is a regular vocational high school English class where students use smartphones, tablets, or computers. Since students have different levels of vocabulary knowledge and speaking ability, the lesson needs to provide simple input, repeated practice, and individualized support.

One major challenge is that many students are not highly motivated by traditional grammar-based English lessons. Some students feel anxious when they are asked to speak English in front of others. Therefore, this project uses familiar content such as pop songs, school-life vocabulary, short expressions, and speech recognition-based speaking practice to make English learning more accessible and less stressful.

---

## 🎵 Lesson Purpose

This lesson teaches students to listen to English, understand simple expressions, and practice speaking basic words and sentences.

The lesson begins with a pop song activity to increase motivation and provide meaningful listening input. Students listen to the song with lyrics or captions, answer simple comprehension questions, learn key expressions, and complete a matching activity.

After the pop song activity, students move to the **Daily English 400** section. They listen to school-life vocabulary, check which words they know or do not know well, and practice speaking selected words or simple sentences through the speaking quiz.

This lesson is meaningful because it connects English learning to students’ interests and daily lives. Instead of starting with difficult grammar explanations or long reading passages, the lesson begins with listening, meaning, and speaking. This helps low-proficiency learners build confidence and experience English as something they can understand and use.

## 🧠 Theoretical Background

This app is designed based on several language learning principles that are closely related to low-proficiency EFL learners’ listening, vocabulary learning, and speaking practice.

### 1. Comprehensible Input

This app reflects the idea of **Comprehensible Input**. Krashen and Terrell (1983) argue that language learners benefit from meaningful input that they can understand. In this app, students first listen to understandable English words, expressions, and song lyrics before moving to speaking practice.

This design is especially suitable for low-proficiency learners because they may feel burdened if they are asked to read long texts, write sentences, or analyze grammar from the beginning. By providing understandable spoken input first, the app helps students become familiar with English sounds in a low-pressure way.

### 2. Reading While Listening (RWL)

The app also uses **Reading While Listening (RWL)**. In the pop song activity, students listen to English while reading lyrics or captions. This helps learners connect spoken English with written forms and meaning.

Hui (2024) found that English learners showed better comprehension in the reading-while-listening condition than in the listening-only condition. This suggests that written support can scaffold listening comprehension. Therefore, the lyrics and captions in this app are not used to replace listening. Instead, they help learners visualize the spoken input and understand it more clearly.

### 3. Audiolingual Practice and Repeated Oral Practice

After listening, students hear a speech model and repeat words or expressions several times. This is related to **audiolingual practice**, especially the idea of repeated oral practice.

For low-proficiency learners, repetition is not simply mechanical drilling. Many students may know the meaning of a word but still have difficulty saying it aloud. Repeated listening and speaking practice helps students become familiar with English sounds, stress, rhythm, and basic sentence patterns.

However, this app does not use repetition only for mechanical memorization. Students repeat words and expressions that can be used in real situations, so the practice is connected to communicative purposes.

### 4. Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)

The app also reflects some principles of **Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)**. The learning content is not organized only around grammar points. Instead, it focuses on useful expressions that students can use in real situations.

For example, students practice expressions for asking for help, introducing themselves, saying what they want, ordering food, asking about places, and talking about school life. Through these activities, students experience English as a tool for communication rather than only as a school subject.

This app does not fully implement CLT in every activity, because many activities are individual listening and speaking practices. However, it reflects CLT principles by focusing on meaningful expressions and real-life use.

### 5. Form-Meaning-Use Vocabulary Learning

The vocabulary activities reflect **Form-Meaning-Use Vocabulary Learning**. Nation (2001) explains that knowing a word involves knowing its form, meaning, and use.

In this app, students do not only memorize the Korean meaning of a word. They listen to the word, check its meaning, pronounce it, and practice it in a simple sentence. For example, students may learn the word `pencil` by listening to its sound, checking its meaning, saying the word, and then using it in a sentence such as `I need a pencil.`

In this way, vocabulary learning becomes connected to listening, pronunciation, meaning, and actual use.

### 6. Mobile/AI-Mediated Interactive Language Practice

The app reflects **mobile and AI-mediated interactive language practice** because students use a digital tool to listen, answer, speak, retry, and receive feedback. Wei (2023) showed that AI-mediated language instruction can be related to English learning achievement, L2 motivation, and self-regulated learning.

In this app, students can learn at their own pace. They can listen again, check answers, retry speaking tasks, and review difficult words. This structure supports repeated practice, self-checking, and more individualized learning opportunities inside and outside the classroom.

### 7. ASR-Based Pronunciation and Self-Paced Speaking Practice

The speaking activities are directly connected to **automatic speech recognition (ASR)-based pronunciation practice**. Neri, Cucchiarini, and Strik (2003) discussed the use of ASR technology for second language pronunciation learning. More recently, Ngo, Chen, and Lai (2024) reported in a meta-analysis that ASR can support ESL/EFL pronunciation development.

In this app, students say words or sentences into the microphone, check whether the app recognizes their speech, and try again if necessary. This allows students to practice speaking at their own pace.

This is useful in a classroom where one teacher cannot immediately check every student’s pronunciation. The app gives each student a chance to speak, receive a recognition result, and retry individually.

### 8. Repeated Word-Level Speaking Practice

The individual word repetition activity is not just mechanical repetition. It is a basic speaking practice that helps students learn the sound form of vocabulary and develop oral automaticity.

Low-proficiency learners may understand the meaning of a word but still struggle to pronounce it. Therefore, they need opportunities to repeatedly listen to target words, say them aloud, and check the speech recognition result.

This repeated speaking practice helps students connect the sound and meaning of English words, reduce speaking anxiety, and prepare for later sentence-level speaking and simple communication.

## 📱 App Purpose

I built this app to support low-proficiency English learners who need more opportunities to listen, repeat, speak, and receive feedback.

In a regular classroom, it is difficult for one teacher to listen to every student’s pronunciation and provide immediate feedback. The app helps solve this problem by giving students individual listening and speaking practice.

The app supports self-paced learning. Students can listen to words and expressions repeatedly, check meanings, speak into the microphone, and see whether their pronunciation is recognized. This allows students to practice without the pressure of speaking in front of the whole class.

The app is not intended to replace the teacher. Instead, it supports teacher-guided instruction by providing repeated input, speaking practice, and immediate feedback.

## 🛠️ App Design

The app is a customized Streamlit-based English learning app. It includes several learning modules, such as:

- world map quiz
- pop song learning activities
- Daily English 400 vocabulary practice
- word-level speaking quiz
- sentence-level speaking practice
- matching games
- short reflection or writing activities

The pop song module includes background learning, lyrics or captions, listening activities, comprehension questions, key expressions, sentence matching, and short reflection activities. This module helps students receive meaningful English input in an enjoyable and low-anxiety context.

The **Daily English 400** module organizes vocabulary into familiar themes, such as school life, classroom activities, home and daily life, hobbies, weather, shopping, and food. In the lesson, students use the school-life vocabulary section.

Learners interact with the app by clicking buttons, listening to audio, checking meanings, selecting answers, matching expressions, marking difficult words, and speaking into the microphone. Students who need basic practice can start with single words. Students who are ready for more challenge can practice simple sentences using the target vocabulary.

## 🏫 Classroom Use

In the 50-minute lesson, the app is used in three main ways.

First, it is used as a warm-up tool through a short world map quiz. This helps students focus and creates an active classroom atmosphere.

Second, it is used for pop song-based listening. Students choose a song, learn background information, listen with lyrics or captions, answer comprehension questions, and practice key expressions. This makes listening more meaningful and engaging.

Third, it is used for vocabulary and speaking practice. Students listen to school-life words, identify words they do not know well, and practice pronouncing selected words in the speaking quiz. Students who are more confident can also practice short sentences using the target vocabulary.

The app improves the lesson by giving students more chances to listen and speak individually. It also helps the teacher identify difficult words and provide focused feedback. Students who need more practice can repeat words, while students who are ready can move on to sentence-level speaking. In this way, the app supports adaptive and self-paced learning.

## ⚠️ Limitations

One current limitation is that speech recognition is not always perfectly accurate. Recognition results can be affected by pronunciation, background noise, microphone quality, or device settings. Because of this, the app’s recognition result should be used as a practice signal rather than as a perfect assessment of pronunciation.

The app supports both word-level and sentence-level speaking practice, but students still need teacher guidance. The teacher should model pronunciation, explain meanings, help students who have difficulty using the microphone, and connect practiced words and sentences to real communication.

---

## 🚀 Future Development

In the future, the app could be improved in several ways.

First, the speech recognition function could become more learner-friendly. It should be flexible enough to accept minor pronunciation differences while still preventing clearly wrong answers from being accepted.

Second, the app could save each student’s difficult words and sentences so that they can review them in later lessons. This would make the learning experience more personalized.

Third, more sentence-level speaking activities could be added. For example, after practicing a word such as `pencil`, students could practice sentences such as `I need a pencil` or `This is my pencil`.

Fourth, the app could include progress tracking so that students and the teacher can see which words and sentences have been practiced, which items were recognized successfully, and which items need more review.

Finally, more pop songs, short readings, school-life expressions, and job-related communication tasks could be added to make the app more useful for vocational high school students.

---

## ✅ Summary

This project supports low-proficiency vocational high school students through pop song listening, caption-supported comprehension, vocabulary learning, and speech recognition-based speaking practice. The app helps students listen, repeat, speak, check, and try again at their own pace.

The lesson is designed to be teacher-guided and app-supported. The teacher introduces the topic, models language, monitors students, and gives feedback, while the app provides repeated input, individualized speaking practice, and immediate feedback. Overall, the project aims to make English learning more interactive, personalized, and confidence-building.
